"Every ChatGPT question uses a bottle of water."

I went and checked. The number is real, and it is also nearly useless.

It comes from a UC Riverside paper: roughly 519 ml for a 100-word email on GPT-4. Other credible estimates put a single exchange at a few drops. That is a 2,000x disagreement, and here is the part worth keeping: both are true, because they are measuring different fences. One counts only the water evaporated inside the building. The other counts the power plant upstream, and the chip fab before that.

A fair answer for one ordinary chatbot exchange today is somewhere between a few drops and a shot glass. Mistral ran the first full independent audit of a model's footprint and landed at 45 ml per page of generated text, training included. Three tablespoons.

For scale, measured the same generous way the scary number is measured:

Cup of coffee, about 140 litres.
Cotton t-shirt, about 2,700 litres.
Hamburger, about 2,500 litres. Sixteen bathtubs.

And the comparison that ends the argument: US golf courses use about 1.5 billion gallons of water a day. Every data centre in America, every cloud and every AI model combined, directly consumed about 17.4 billion gallons in all of 2023. Golf out-drinks the entire American internet roughly thirty times over.

So the panic is misdirected. Here is why I did not stop there.

The water is not destroyed. It is displaced. Evaporative cooling consumptively removes 70 to 90 percent of what it withdraws from the local watershed, hands it to the sky, and the rain falls somewhere else. "Consumed" on a water bill never meant gone from Earth. It meant gone from here.

Which is how a data centre can be 0.02 percent of a country's water withdrawals and still be a real problem for one specific town. National averages do not drink. Local aquifers do.

Both of those are true at the same time, and almost everyone arguing about this is carrying only one of them.
